\chapter{Motion as Life: Reality Is Fundamentally Dynamic}
\label{ch:motion-as-life}

\epigraph{Creation is the expression of motion. Motion is life.}{\AbdulBaha,
  \textit{The Promulgation of Universal Peace}}

\firstword{T}{here} is a temptation, when thinking about the physical world, to imagine that rest is the default. Things exist, and then they move. Motion is something that happens \emph{to} them — a state they enter and exit. Left alone, they stop. The resting state, the motionless state, is the natural one. Energy is something added to a system; its absence is what the world tends toward.

This intuition, I want to argue, mistakes a limit case for the fundamental reality. Physics began to take it apart long before quantum mechanics: Galileo and Newton taught that a body left alone keeps whatever uniform motion it has, and that whether it counts as moving or resting depends on the frame of reference from which it is described. Quantum mechanics goes further, in a way that has to be stated with care because it is easy to overstate. The claim I will defend is that rest in the sense the intuition has in mind, a thing sitting still with all its properties fixed and definite, is not what we find at the foundations of the world. What looks like rest in everyday life is the steadiness of patterns maintained by ongoing activity, and beneath those patterns there is no floor of inert, fully definite stuff.

Three traditions arrive at this conclusion by entirely different routes. Physics arrives through the mathematics of quantum uncertainty: from the commutation relation between position and momentum it follows that no state gives a particle both a sharp position and a sharp momentum, so rest in the classical sense is unavailable in principle. Process philosophy arrives through metaphysical reflection on what it means for anything to be real at all, in Whitehead's argument that becoming, not substance, is the fundamental category of existence. The \bahai writings arrive through a direct statement that is as simple as it is radical: ``Creation is the expression of motion. Motion is life'' \citep[Talk 52]{PUP}.

This is the sixth and final convergence I will document in this book, and in some ways it is the most fundamental. The first five established specific structural features of reality — relational ontology, nonlocal interconnection, hierarchy of comprehension, the dynamics of coherence and decomposition, the whole encoded in each part. This one reaches beneath them to a more basic question: what kind of thing is reality? Is it fundamentally a collection of static things, or fundamentally a process? Is being prior to becoming, or is becoming what being consists of?

All three traditions answer that becoming is prior. To be is to be in process, and rest, where we find it, is a feature of patterns within that process rather than the ground beneath it.

I want to make this precise, because the claim is stranger than it sounds, and because the physics in particular is easy to state wrongly.


\section{The Physics: Zero-Point Energy and the End of Classical Rest}
\label{sec:ch12-physics}

Let me begin with what quantum mechanics actually says about the possibility of rest, because the result is both precise and philosophically significant.

In classical mechanics, a system can in principle be brought to perfect rest. Cool a gas and its molecules slow down; temperature measures their average kinetic energy, and in principle that average can be driven to zero. Absolute zero, zero kelvin or about minus 273 degrees Celsius, is where that average would reach zero, and in the classical picture an object at absolute zero is, quite literally, perfectly still.

Quantum mechanics does not allow this, although what exactly it rules out needs to be stated with some care.

The reason is Heisenberg's uncertainty principle \citep{Heisenberg1927}. In its simplest form, it says that you cannot know a particle's position and its momentum at once with perfect precision: the more precisely one is fixed, the less precisely the other can be. Better instruments would not help. On the standard reading, the limit is a feature of quantum reality itself: no quantum state assigns definite values to position and momentum at the same time.

The consequence for rest follows directly. Rest, as classical physics pictures it, means a body at a definite place with a definite momentum of zero, which is exactly the combination the uncertainty principle excludes. A particle with a perfectly definite momentum has a completely indefinite position, spread in some sense across all of space; a particle with a perfectly definite position has a completely indefinite momentum and an infinite average kinetic energy. No quantum state answers to the classical picture of a particle sitting still at a point.

Notice what this does and does not rule out. A free particle can have a momentum very close to zero, provided it is spread over a correspondingly large region. What is forbidden is stillness at a place, and the particles that make up ordinary matter are held in place: electrons in atoms, atoms in molecules and crystals.

The mathematical statement is $\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \geq \hbar/2$. Here $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ are the spreads (technically, the standard deviations) of the results you would get by measuring position, or momentum, on many identically prepared systems, and $\hbar$, Planck's constant divided by $2\pi$, is tiny but not zero. Drive $\Delta x$ toward zero and $\Delta p$ must grow without limit, and the average kinetic energy with it. Every degree of confinement has a price: the tighter the confinement, the larger the spread in momentum and the kinetic energy that comes with it.

This price is the origin of what physicists call zero-point energy. Cool a confined quantum system into its ground state, the state of lowest possible energy, and its energy still lies above the bottom of the potential well that holds it. No further cooling removes the excess, because there is no lower state to fall into. It is the cost of confinement under the uncertainty principle, and it has nothing to do with heat.

\subsection*{A Worked Example: The Stillest State of an Oscillator}

The simplest case shows both what zero-point energy is and what it is not, and it corrects a picture that popular accounts often get wrong.

Picture a particle held by a spring, or, more realistically, an atom in a crystal, pulled back toward its resting place with a force that grows in proportion to how far it strays. Physicists call this a harmonic oscillator. Classically, its lowest-energy state is obvious: the particle sits motionless at the bottom of the well, with zero energy.

Quantum mechanically, the particle cannot sit at the bottom with zero momentum, so the question is what the best available compromise is. Suppose its position is spread over some width around the bottom. Two costs compete. Squeeze the width, and the uncertainty principle forces a larger spread in momentum, raising the kinetic energy; let the width grow, and the particle is more often found up the sides of the well, raising the potential energy. Between the two lies a best width, which a short calculation locates. At the optimum the two costs are equal, each a quarter of $\hbar\omega$, where $\omega$ is the oscillator's natural frequency, for a total of $\hbar\omega/2$.\footnote{For readers who want the algebra: with $m$ the particle's mass, the uncertainty principle makes the average kinetic energy at least $\hbar^2/(8m\,\Delta x^2)$, and the average potential energy is at least $m\omega^2\Delta x^2/2$. The sum is smallest when $\Delta x^2 = \hbar/(2m\omega)$, where each term equals $\hbar\omega/4$. Because the bound holds for every state, no state of the oscillator has an average energy below $\hbar\omega/2$.} That is exactly the energy of the true ground state, which has exactly this optimal shape: a bell curve centered on the bottom of the well, whose spreads in position and momentum multiply to the smallest value the uncertainty principle allows. Temperature plays no part in the argument. The half quantum of energy is simply what it costs to be confined.

Now ask what the particle is doing in this state. Here the popular picture, in which the ground-state oscillator trembles forever with a tiny vibration, goes wrong. The ground state is what physicists call a stationary state, and the name is exact: nothing measurable about it changes with time. The probability of finding the particle in any region is the same at every moment; its average position stays at the bottom of the well, its average momentum stays at zero, and the spreads neither grow nor shrink. It does not swing back and forth, not even by a little. What is not zero is the spread. Measure the momentum and you will find some value other than zero, now in one direction, now in the other; repeat the preparation and the measurement many times, and the results scatter around zero with an average squared momentum that is not zero. That average is what the ground state's kinetic energy measures. Physicists call this zero-point motion or zero-point fluctuation, and the names are standard, but what they name is a spread in the possible results of measurement; no hidden trembling along a path is implied.

A comparison makes the difference concrete. A classical particle with the same energy would swing between two turning points, slowing near each, so that caught at a random moment it would most likely be near one of the ends, and it could never be found beyond them. The quantum ground state reverses this: the most likely place is the middle, the likelihood tapers smoothly on either side, and there is roughly one chance in six of finding the particle beyond the classical turning points, even though the ground state's energy is perfectly sharp. No collection of classical oscillators all having exactly that energy could produce this shape.

Zero-point energy is nevertheless physically real, and its consequences can be large. Helium atoms are light and attract one another only weakly, so the zero-point energy that would come with pinning each atom to a place in a crystal outweighs the attraction that would hold the crystal together. Helium at ordinary pressure therefore stays liquid down to the lowest temperatures ever reached, and it solidifies only under substantial pressure. The same balance is why atoms do not collapse: squeezing an electron closer to its nucleus would lower its potential energy but raise its kinetic energy by more. In both cases the work is done by the price of localization, paid by a state that does not change.

The same analysis carries over to quantum fields, where the popular picture goes most badly astray. In quantum field theory, particles are excitations of underlying fields; a photon, for instance, is a quantum of the electromagnetic field. That field, in empty space, can be analyzed exactly as a vast collection of independent oscillators, one for each pattern of vibration it can support, and the vacuum is the state in which every one of them is in its ground state. So the vacuum is stationary; indeed, it looks the same to every observer moving at constant velocity. The average field is zero everywhere and stays zero. What is not zero is the spread: measurements of the field in a small region would scatter around zero, and the smaller the region, the wider the scatter. Those spreads are what physicists mean by vacuum fluctuations. The image of particle pairs popping into existence and vanishing again comes from diagrams used to organize certain calculations, in which such pairs appear as intermediate terms. It is a picturesque shorthand for those terms and does not describe events in empty space. Real particles can be created out of the vacuum only when something supplies the energy.

Summed over all the oscillators, the zero-point energy of a field is formally infinite, but outside gravity only differences in energy have observable effects. The Casimir effect is the best-known such difference. In 1948 Hendrik Casimir predicted that two uncharged metal plates, placed parallel and very close together in vacuum, would attract one another, and the force has since been measured. The textbook derivation attributes it to the change in the field's zero-point energy as the plates restrict which patterns of vibration fit between them. The same force can also be derived, without mentioning vacuum energy, as an attraction between the charges and currents in the two plates, a long-range relative of the van der Waals forces between molecules. What the effect shows, on either reading, is that quantum fields and the matter they couple to have measurable properties even in their lowest states, and that those properties depend on the boundaries placed in the field. Neither reading makes the vacuum a churning sea.

\subsection*{What the Physics Does and Does Not Say}

What this means philosophically needs more care than the popular story gives it. Follow physical explanation downward, from objects to molecules to atoms to particles to fields, and you never arrive at classical stillness, a state in which things sit at definite places with zero momentum. Nor do you arrive at a seething agitation. The lowest states of quantum systems are stationary; what they cannot be is sharp. The accurate conclusion is narrower than the slogan that nothing is ever at rest: the kind of rest classical physics imagined describes no quantum state at all.

Classical rest belongs to the level of large objects, where quantum effects wash out. A stone on a table is at rest in exactly that sense, yet it is anything but inert. At room temperature its atoms vibrate about their places, and the stone continually absorbs and emits thermal radiation and is struck by molecules of air; its constancy is a steady state of exchange with its surroundings. Cooled all the way into its ground state, it would have nothing left to exchange, but its atoms would still not settle into classical rest. They would remain spread, in a state that no longer changes.

The inference from the mathematics to these conclusions is rigorous. The commutation relation between position and momentum yields the uncertainty relation as a theorem, and the uncertainty relation yields both the exclusion of classical rest and, for any confined system, a ground-state energy above the classical minimum. The further step, to the claim that reality is constitutively dynamic, is philosophical rather than mathematical. I think it is the right step, but it is a step, and I will return at the end of the chapter to the strongest reason for doubting it.


\section{The Process-Philosophical View: Process All the Way Down}
\label{sec:ch12-process}

Alfred North Whitehead arrived at a closely related conclusion from a direction so different that the agreement itself demands explanation.

Whitehead was a mathematician who became a philosopher, and his central metaphysical work, \textit{Process and Reality}, is one of the most ambitious philosophical systems of the twentieth century \citep{Whitehead1929}. His starting point was not quantum mechanics, though he was aware of the new physics, but dissatisfaction with the standard account of what things are, often called the philosophy of substance: the view that the world consists, at bottom, of enduring things that possess properties and stand in relations. On this view, a stone is a thing that has hardness, shape, mass, and color; the properties belong to the stone, and the stone persists through time as the same thing while its properties change. Substance is prior; properties and relations are secondary.

Whitehead's argument is that this gets the ontological order exactly backwards. His alternative, which he called the ``philosophy of organism'' and which has come to be known as process philosophy, holds that the fundamental units of reality are not enduring substances but momentary occasions of becoming. And these occasions are creative: each adds to the universe something that was not already present in its antecedents.

The technical term for these fundamental units is ``actual occasions'' or ``actual entities.'' An actual occasion is not a thing that goes through a process; it \emph{is} a process of becoming. Its being consists entirely in its becoming. It comes into existence through what Whitehead calls ``concrescence'' — a process of gathering together the relational inheritances from prior occasions and synthesizing them into a new, unified, definite actuality. Once that process of synthesis is complete, the occasion reaches what Whitehead calls its ``satisfaction'' — its definite character — and it perishes. It does not persist as an enduring thing; it becomes part of the objective past available to be inherited by subsequent occasions. What we experience as an enduring object is what Whitehead calls a ``society'' of actual occasions: a pattern of successive occasions that inherit certain characteristics from one another and thereby maintain a stable identity through time.

The central point is that there are no static actual occasions. Even the most apparently inert physical object, a rock or a grain of dust or an electron in the ground state of an atom, is on the process account a pattern of micro-events of becoming, each arising and perishing, each inheriting from its predecessors and contributing to its successors. The rock's stability is real, but it is the stability of a pattern, more like that of a flame than the persistence of a lump of stuff. A flame keeps its form through continuous combustion, never made of the same matter from moment to moment. The stone, on the process account, is the same kind of thing, at a scale of temporal resolution so fine that we cannot perceive the becoming that maintains its solidity.

The flame analogy needs one physical correction. A flame must draw in fuel and oxygen continuously to keep its shape, and it is made of different matter from one moment to the next; a stone keeps its shape near equilibrium, with the same atoms, for millions of years. So the process claim concerns something other than hidden burning. Even the persistence of the same atoms is, on Whitehead's analysis, an achievement: each moment of an atom's existence is a new occasion inheriting the pattern of its predecessor, and sameness over time is the name for inheritance that succeeds.

This connects directly to creativity, which Whitehead identifies as ``the universal of universals characterizing ultimate matter of fact'' \citep[p.~21]{Whitehead1929}. Creativity names the character of reality as such: every new actual occasion is new, and the universe at every moment adds to itself something not already fully determined by what came before. This holds at every scale, for the collision of two particles as much as for a human decision. Within the framework, then, stasis is a logical impossibility: an occasion just is a process of becoming, and a static process of becoming is a contradiction in terms. For anything actual, to be is to become, and to finish becoming is to perish.

Stability is never mere repetition. Each new occasion in the society that constitutes a rock inherits the pattern of its predecessors but synthesizes that inheritance in its own act of becoming. The rock's stability is the recurrence of a creative pattern, and even the most inert object is, at the level of its fundamental description, an ongoing creative process.

This is Whitehead's deepest challenge to common sense and to the philosophy of substance: the denial that any actual thing simply \emph{is}, without also \emph{becoming}. He held the contrary view responsible for persistent confusions in philosophy, among them the mind-body problem and the difficulty of explaining genuine novelty, all arising from mistaking the abstract (the enduring substance, extracted from the process that constitutes it) for the concrete (the actual occasion, which is nothing other than its process of becoming).

One detail of Whitehead's scheme matters for everything that follows, and it is easy to miss: the basic units of his world do not move. An actual occasion comes into being and perishes. It does not travel from place to place, and it does not change, because nothing in it persists through a change. Whitehead is explicit that motion and change describe relations among occasions, the differences between successive members of a society \citep{Whitehead1929}. A moving electron is a route of occasions, each of which is where it is and becomes what it becomes. The process Whitehead makes fundamental is becoming, and his name for the ultimate is creativity; locomotion is derivative. His scheme even contains items that never become: the eternal objects, pure potentials for the determination of fact, which occasions take up or exclude but which never themselves arise or perish. What Whitehead rules out is anything actual that is static.

Set beside the physics, Whitehead's account and the uncertainty principle deny the same thing: that the world bottoms out in items that are simply there, fully definite and inert. They deny it for different reasons and in different senses, and I will have to reckon with that difference at the end of the chapter. But the shared denial is real, and common sense would not have led anyone to expect it.


\section{The Bahá'í View: Motion Is Life}
\label{sec:ch12-bahai}

The \bahai tradition arrives at this same conclusion through a third route, and it states the conclusion with a directness that neither the physics nor the philosophy quite matches.

The key text opens a talk that \AbdulBaha gave at Tremont Temple in Boston on 22 May 1912, to a Unitarian conference. Its English text, compiled from stenographic notes, is preserved in \textit{The Promulgation of Universal Peace}:

\begin{quote}
``Creation is the expression of motion. Motion is life. A moving object is a living object, whereas that which is motionless and inert is as dead. All created forms are progressive in their planes, or kingdoms of existence, under the stimulus of the power or spirit of life. The universal energy is dynamic. Nothing is stationary in the material world of outer phenomena or in the inner world of intellect and consciousness.'' \citep[Talk 52]{PUP}
\end{quote}

The first thing to notice is the passage's grammar, with one caution: \AbdulBaha spoke in Persian through an interpreter, so no argument should hang on a single English copula. Still, the force of the opening sentences survives any reasonable allowance for translation. He does not say that creation involves motion, or that living things tend to move. He says that creation is the expression of motion and that motion is life, and the rest of the passage keeps to that strength: a moving thing is a living thing, and nothing, outer or inner, is stationary. The claim concerns what creation is, which is stronger than any claim about a property that created things happen to have.

In philosophical form, the statement has the same shape as the inversion Whitehead would set out seventeen years later: motion is what existence consists of, with no motionless substrate beneath it. Its negative half needs more careful reading than it usually gets: ``that which is motionless and inert is as dead.'' The word ``as'' matters. The passage likens the motionless to the dead; it does not say that the motionless is dead, still less that it does not exist. In the Evanston talk discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:decoherence-decomposition}, given less than three weeks earlier, \AbdulBaha insists that ``nonexistence is only relative'': what we call nonexistent is a lower degree of being measured against a higher one \citep[Talk 38]{PUP}. Inertness, on this reading, is a diminished degree of participation in life, a long way from an exit from being.

The scope is wide. Nothing is stationary ``in the material world of outer phenomena or in the inner world of intellect and consciousness,'' so biology, mechanics, and the inner life of the mind all fall within the claim. And where one familiar picture holds that a thing either exists or does not, the passage, read with the Evanston talk, suggests that existence admits of degrees that track participation in the dynamic of creation. The motionless and inert ``is as dead'': not nonexistent, but at the low end of a scale whose other end is life in its fullest sense.

This connects to another passage that establishes the same principle from the negative side. In a talk in Chicago on 5 May 1912, \AbdulBaha speaks of what happens when relational cohesion fails:

\begin{quote}
``[E]very contingent and phenomenal being is a composition of distinct elements. As long as there is affinity and cohesion among these constituent elements, strength and life are manifest; but when dissension and repulsion arise among them, disintegration follows.'' \citep[Talk 41]{PUP}
\end{quote}

This passage describes what we might call the dynamic theory of integrated being: a thing is constituted by the ongoing relational process of its parts maintaining affinity with one another, a pattern of relational activity rather than a set of parts assembled once and left to persist. When the affinity among constituent elements dissipates, the thing does not merely change state. It ceases to be the thing it was. The form perishes.

This is the decomposition side of the motion-as-life principle, and Chapter~\ref{ch:decoherence-decomposition} traced its physical counterpart in detail. When a quantum system's coherence disperses into its environment, the relational organization that made it a quantum whole is what dissolves, while the underlying information is conserved. As I argued there, what the physical and the metaphysical processes share is abstract form rather than mechanism: in both, an integrated entity is sustained by ongoing relational activity, and the failure of that activity is what its dissolution consists in.

The motion-as-life passage from Talk 52 also connects to something that \AbdulBaha says about the creative role of love. In Talk 48, he makes the claim I have already quoted in earlier chapters, but its connection to the motion-as-life principle deserves fresh attention:

\begin{quote}
``All created things are expressions of the affinity and cohesion of elementary substances, and nonexistence is the absence of their attraction and agreement. \ldots\ Everything partakes of this nature and is subject to this principle, for the creative foundation in all its degrees and kingdoms is an expression or outcome of love.'' \citep[Talk 48]{PUP}
\end{quote}

Read together with the motion-as-life passage, these two texts suggest a connection that I take to be the heart of the \bahai contribution to this convergence. Motion is life; creation is the expression of motion; and the creative foundation, in all its degrees, is an expression of love. I read this as saying that the dynamism through which creation expresses itself has love, \mahabbat, as its ground. That is my reading, and it goes well beyond anything physics could confirm. Zero-point energy has a complete explanation within quantum mechanics, and nothing in that explanation mentions affinity or love. What the physics offers the reading is narrower: the classical picture of inert rest, the nearest physical counterpart to what the passage calls motionless and inert, describes no quantum state.

This is the \bahai tradition's distinctive contribution. The physics establishes that classical rest is unavailable at the fundamental level, and the process philosophy argues that becoming is fundamental; the \bahai writings, on the reading I have given, name the character of that becoming as love, the relational affinity and cohesion through which existence maintains itself at every scale.

\section{A Direct Convergence, Stated Carefully}
\label{sec:ch12-convergence}

In the companion \bahai studies paper I called this the most direct of the six convergences \citep{AdamsMahabbat}, and on the surface it is. In the other five there is interpretive work to be done before the shared structure comes into view. Here the three claims can be set side by side in nearly the same words.

Quantum physics says that rest in the classical sense is unavailable. No state gives a particle a sharp position and a sharp momentum, no confined system can sink to the classical minimum of its energy, and even the ground state and the vacuum keep a zero-point energy and nonzero spreads.

Process philosophy says that becoming is the fundamental category. Actual occasions are processes of becoming, creativity is the ultimate category, and nothing actual is static, because an occasion that was not becoming would not be an occasion at all.

The \bahai writings say: ``Creation is the expression of motion. Motion is life. \ldots\ that which is motionless and inert is as dead'' \citep[Talk 52]{PUP}.

The three statements come from different directions: the mathematics of quantities that do not commute, reflection on what it is to be an actual entity, and a claim about the nature of creation. Each denies that rest is the ground of being on which motion occasionally occurs, and each treats the rest we observe as the steadiness of a pattern within process. That reversal runs against common sense, which takes rest as natural and motion as needing explanation, and I do not think it is a coincidence that three unrelated inquiries make it.

This is the sixth and final structural convergence of the book, and its position is not accidental. The first five established specific features of a relational universe. The sixth concerns the universe in which those features obtain: a universe of processes, of events of becoming, in which those features belong to everything that exists.

\subsection*{The Strongest Objection, and What the Convergence Does Not Claim}

The strongest objection is that this convergence rests on a pun. The word ``motion'' does different work in each of the three places I have found it, and once the senses are separated, the agreement looks verbal.

In the physics, the relevant fact is not motion in any everyday sense. The worked example showed that the lowest state of a quantum system is stationary; what quantum mechanics forbids is sharpness, and calling that restlessness is a courtesy. The point can be pressed further. On Bohmian mechanics, an interpretation that reproduces the standard predictions, a particle in the ground state of an oscillator is literally at rest: it sits at a definite position with zero velocity, and the spread in momentum appears only when a measurement disturbs it. So even the modest claim that quantum particles are never still depends on how the theory is interpreted. In Whitehead, as I noted, the basic units do not move at all; motion belongs to the relations among them. And in Talk 52, ``motion'' is a word about life and progress. The passage speaks of created forms that are progressive in their kingdoms and extends its claim to the inner world of intellect and consciousness. In the sentences that follow, \AbdulBaha applies the principle to religion: a religion that no longer moves and progresses has lost its divine life, he argues, and so divine revelation must itself keep moving forward. The sense of motion the talk goes on to use is development, and momentum does not enter into it. Put the three senses side by side, the objector concludes, and what they share is a word.

I think the objection is correct about the words, and the fair response is to concede that point in full. No single sense of ``motion'' runs through the three traditions. Anyone who read Talk 52 as a statement about momentum, or the uncertainty principle as a statement about life, would be making a mistake. This is why, in the companion philosophy paper, I placed this convergence nearer the analogical end of the six: the level of abstraction at which the three claims agree does more work here than it does in the first four convergences \citep{AdamsThreePaths}.

What survives the concession is a shared denial with definite content. Each tradition denies that the world bottoms out in items that are simply there, fully determinate and complete in themselves. The physics denies it for every confined system and for the vacuum. Whitehead denies it for everything actual; his occasions do not move, but he locates motion in their succession, and nothing actual in his scheme is static. \AbdulBaha denies it for the whole created order, outer and inner, and his application to religion depends on the general premise and so does not narrow it, since the argument runs from the claim that nothing created is stationary to a conclusion about religion in particular. Even Bohmian mechanics, which restores a particle at rest, keeps it there only through a guiding wave that, in any system of more than one particle, is defined over the configuration of all of them at once (Chapter~\ref{ch:qm-requires-process}). What sits still in that picture is not a self-contained thing.

The three denials are not one denial seen three times. Physics denies sharpness, Whitehead denies static actuality, and \AbdulBaha denies that anything created stands still. What they share is a target, the inert and self-sufficient thing that common sense takes as the unit of reality, and that target is specific enough to exclude a good deal: classical mechanics permits exactly that kind of rest, and so does the everyday picture with which this chapter began. The convergence is real at the level of the denial. It is weaker at the level of what each tradition puts in the denied picture's place, and I have tried not to claim more agreement there than the texts support.

With that narrowing in place, here is what the convergence does not claim.

\begin{itemize}
\item It does not claim that quantum systems tremble ceaselessly, that empty space churns with particles, or that zero-point energy is a hidden reservoir of motion that could be tapped for power; a system in its ground state has no lower state to give its energy up to.
\item It does not claim that Talk 52 describes the uncertainty principle, zero-point energy, or any other piece of physics.
\item It does not claim that \AbdulBaha held Whitehead's metaphysics of momentary occasions. In the Evanston talk he speaks of elements that pass from one composition to another without being altered \citep[Talk 38]{PUP}, which is not Whitehead's picture.
\item It does not claim that physics shows the universe to be alive, or that the life of which Talk 52 speaks could be measured.
\end{itemize}

Part IV, which begins in the next chapter, asks what kind of framework is adequate to all six convergences together. It argues that the convergences themselves call for a particular paradigm, and Chapter~\ref{ch:rct} introduces the formal piece of it: a mathematical representation of the relational principle the convergences keep pointing at.

\begin{convergencemoment}{Motion as Life: Reality Is Fundamentally Dynamic}
In quantum mechanics~\citep{Heisenberg1927}, no state gives a particle both a sharp position and a sharp momentum, so the classical picture of rest, a body at a definite place with zero momentum, describes no quantum state.
Confined systems keep a zero-point energy even in their ground state.
That state is stationary, since nothing measurable in it changes, yet its position and momentum remain spread, and the vacuum of quantum field theory has the same character.

In process philosophy, Whitehead makes creativity ``the universal of universals characterizing ultimate matter of fact''~\citep[p.~21]{Whitehead1929}.
Actual occasions are processes of becoming, nothing actual is static, and what appears as stability is a pattern inherited from occasion to occasion.

In the \bahai writings: ``Creation is the expression of motion. Motion is life. A moving object is a living object, whereas that which is motionless and inert is as dead. \ldots\ The universal energy is dynamic. Nothing is stationary''~\citep[Talk 52]{PUP}.
The claim covers the whole created order, outer and inner.

One structure, reached through three different senses of ``motion'': each tradition denies that the world bottoms out in inert things, fully definite and complete in themselves.
The rest we observe is the steadiness of patterns within process.
