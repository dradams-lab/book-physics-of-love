\chapter{Holographic Order: The Whole in Every Part}
\label{ch:holographic-order}

% \epigraph{The whole is implicit in each part.}{David Bohm}

\firstword{H}{ere} is the puzzle.
Take a holographic photograph --- not the flat image but the genuine hologram, recorded on photographic film using coherent laser light.
If you cut that hologram in half and illuminate either piece, you do not see half the original scene.
You see the whole scene, at somewhat lower resolution.
Cut it in quarters.
Each quarter still shows the whole.
Cut it into tiny fragments.
Each fragment still encodes the complete image.

This is strange.
In an ordinary photograph, cutting it in half gives you half the information.
The information is localized: this corner of the photograph stores this portion of the image.
But in a hologram, the information about the whole scene is distributed throughout the recording medium.
Every point on the hologram's surface carries interference patterns that encode the whole scene.
The information is not localized.
It is distributed globally, available at every local point.

This is not a metaphor.
It is a physical fact about how holograms work, rooted in the mathematics of wave interference and Fourier transforms.
The whole is literally present in every part.

The precision matters, because the optical case has a price.
A fragment of a hologram does not reproduce the scene as well as the whole plate does.
The fragment acts like a smaller window onto the recorded wavefront.
Through a smaller window you still see the entire scene, but the image is less sharp, and you can no longer move your head as far to look around the objects in it.
Nothing of the scene has been cut away; what has been lost is resolution and range of perspective.
So the precise optical claim is this: every part carries the whole, and what the size of the part governs is the fidelity with which the whole can be recovered.
Keep that distinction between presence and fidelity in view.
Each of the three traditions in this chapter turns out to need it.

David Bohm saw this and recognized it as a clue to something deep about the structure of reality~\citep{Bohm1980}.
A little more than a decade after Bohm's book appeared, two theoretical physicists working on a quite different problem, the fate of information in black holes, borrowed the same word for a related but distinct idea about how much the boundary of a region can say about its interior~\citep{tHooft1993,Susskind1995}.
And the \bahai writings, engaging neither holography nor quantum gravity, articulate a structurally parallel claim about the presence of the whole within every human soul.

This chapter takes up what I have been calling the fifth convergence --- holographic order, or the principle that the whole is present in every part.
I want to be honest about the complexity here from the start.
This is the most analogical of the six convergences.
The physical ideas involved --- Bohm's implicate order and the holographic principle of quantum gravity --- are distinct from each other, and neither maps onto the \bahai claims with the directness of, say, the decoherence convergence.
But the structural parallel is real.
And in a book that is arguing for structural isomorphism rather than superficial resemblance, the appropriate response to a subtler case is not to omit it but to state it carefully, with appropriate acknowledgment of where the analogy thins.

\section{The Physics: Bohm's Implicate Order and the Holographic Principle}
\label{sec:ch11-physics}

\subsection*{Part A: Bohm's Implicate Order}

David Bohm came to his concept of the implicate order through a kind of productive frustration with standard quantum mechanics.
In 1952 he had published a hidden-variables reformulation of quantum mechanics built around what he called the quantum potential, a line of work he revisited in the essays later collected in \emph{Wholeness and the Implicate Order}~\citep{Bohm1980}, and he was deeply dissatisfied with the view --- dominant in Copenhagen-style interpretations --- that quantum mechanics had reached the limits of what physics could coherently say about reality.

His central objection was to the assumption of fragmentation.
Standard physics treats the world as composed of separate parts that interact locally.
Particles are here or there; fields carry influences from one point to another; everything reduces to the behavior of local elements.
Bohm thought the quantum phenomena --- entanglement, nonlocality, the strange way that quantum systems seem to be intrinsically connected regardless of distance --- suggested that this fragmentation was imposed by our way of looking at things rather than being a feature of reality itself.

His response was to propose a two-level description of nature.
The explicate order is the world as we ordinarily encounter it: objects with definite positions, particles with definite properties, things that appear localized and separate.
But the explicate order is not fundamental.
It is, in Bohm's language, an unfolding --- a local manifestation --- of a deeper structure that he called the implicate order.

In the implicate order, everything is enfolded in everything else.
Not metaphorically, but in a precise sense that Bohm tried to make rigorous using the mathematics of the holographic process.
The interference patterns on a hologram are the analogy: just as every point on the hologram encodes the whole scene through its particular interference pattern, every point in the implicate order encodes the whole universe through its particular structure.
The local is not a fragment of the global; it is a specific unfolding of the whole, at this particular location, in this particular relational context.

To illustrate the intuition, Bohm described an experiment with a rotating cylinder filled with glycerine, a highly viscous fluid that does not mix easily~\citep{Bohm1980}.
Imagine dropping a droplet of ink into the glycerine and slowly rotating the outer cylinder.
The ink stretches out into a thin, invisible thread, seemingly dispersed throughout the fluid.
If you then rotate the cylinder back, the thread retraces its path and the ink droplet reappears, apparently from nowhere.
The droplet was implicate --- enfolded in the fluid --- and becomes explicate again through the reverse rotation.

This is the physical intuition.
The explicate manifestation is a particular unfolding of what is enfolded in the implicate order.
Different acts of unfolding produce different explicate manifestations.
But in each case, the information about the whole is present in the implicate structure; it is not lost when something becomes enfolded.

The glycerine device should be taken for what it is.
It is a classical demonstration, and it works because slow, viscous flow of this kind is very nearly reversible: the fluid layers slide past one another without turbulent mixing, so running the motion backward undoes it.
It proves nothing about quantum mechanics.
Its value is conceptual.
It shows that something can be thoroughly spread out, present everywhere in a region and visible nowhere, without having been destroyed, and that a suitable operation on the whole can bring it back into local, explicit form.
Bohm used it to loosen the grip of a habit of thought: the assumption that whatever is real must be located somewhere in particular.

The mathematical underpinning is the algebra of the holomovement --- Bohm's term for the dynamic, flowing process that is the fundamental reality, from which both implicate and explicate orders are abstractions.
The holomovement cannot itself be directly described, because any description fragments it into aspects.
But the two orders --- implicate and explicate --- provide a way of talking about how the unfragmented whole gives rise to the apparently fragmented local world we inhabit.

The implications are radical.
Quantum nonlocality, in Bohm's framework, is not strange or paradoxical: it is what you expect when two particles are both expressions of the same implicate order.
Their correlation across space is not a mysterious action at a distance; it is the reflection of their common enfolding in a deeper reality that does not divide by distance.
Locality --- the idea that things are fundamentally separate and interact only by contact or by signals that propagate through space --- is a feature of the explicate order, not of the implicate order that underlies it.

Two things bearing Bohm's name need to be kept apart here.
The first is the 1952 pilot-wave theory, often called Bohmian mechanics, in which particles have definite positions at all times and are guided by the wave function.
That theory is fully specified for nonrelativistic quantum mechanics and reproduces the standard predictions; its status is that of a complete interpretation, empirically equivalent to the textbook theory, which some physicists and philosophers of physics prefer and many do not.
The second is the implicate order, which Bohm developed from the late 1960s onward and which is a much broader and much less finished program.
It is a proposal about what kind of order the quantum formalism is pointing toward, and Bohm and his collaborators made several attempts to give it algebraic form.
It does not, as it stands, yield new predictions that distinguish it from standard quantum mechanics, and it is not part of the working physics taught in graduate courses.
When I draw on the implicate order in this chapter, I am drawing on a serious physicist's metaphysical proposal, not on an established result.

\subsection*{Part B: The Holographic Principle}

The holographic principle emerged in the 1990s from a completely different problem: the physics of black holes.
Its roots go back to the early 1970s, when Jacob Bekenstein argued that a black hole must carry entropy, and that this entropy must be proportional to the area of its event horizon rather than to the volume the horizon encloses.
Shortly afterward Stephen Hawking showed, by applying quantum field theory in the curved spacetime near a horizon, that black holes radiate with a thermal spectrum --- they emit what is now called Hawking radiation and slowly evaporate.
His calculation fixed the constant that Bekenstein's argument had left open.
In the resulting Bekenstein--Hawking formula, the entropy of a black hole is one quarter of its horizon area $A$, measured in units of the Planck area $\ell_P^{2}$, a patch of roughly $10^{-70}$ square meters built from Newton's constant, Planck's constant, and the speed of light:
\[
S_{\mathrm{BH}} = \frac{k_B\, A}{4\,\ell_P^{2}}, \qquad \ell_P^{2} = \frac{G\hbar}{c^{3}}.
\]
The surprise is in the scaling.
For every ordinary system, entropy grows with volume: double the amount of gas in a box and you double its entropy.
A black hole's entropy grows with the area of its surface.

The formula sharpened an older worry.
When matter falls into a black hole, what happens to the information it carried?
If Hawking radiation is exactly thermal, it carries no information about what fell in.
Then when the black hole has evaporated completely, the information content of that matter would be permanently destroyed, which violates a fundamental principle of quantum mechanics: unitarity, which requires that quantum information is conserved.

Gerard 't~Hooft in 1993 and Leonard Susskind in 1995 took the area law as a clue about quantum gravity in general~\citep{tHooft1993,Susskind1995}.
The reasoning runs roughly like this.
Suppose some region of space held more entropy than a black hole whose horizon coincided with the region's boundary.
Add matter until the region collapses into a black hole of that size.
Its entropy would then have to go down, contradicting the second law of thermodynamics as generalized to include black holes.
So the most information any region can hold is set by the area of its boundary, not by its volume: of order one bit for every few Planck areas, or roughly $10^{69}$ bits per square meter.
't~Hooft and Susskind then took a further step.
They proposed that the bound is a sign of something structural: a complete description of what happens inside a region should be writable in terms of degrees of freedom that live on its boundary, one dimension down.
For a black hole, this means the information about everything that has fallen in is, in principle, encoded at the horizon rather than hidden in an inaccessible interior.

This is the holographic principle, and the name is itself an analogy.
As a flat photographic plate stores a three-dimensional scene, a two-dimensional boundary would store the physics of a three-dimensional volume.
If the principle holds generally, the implications are extraordinary.
The volume we move around in would not be the fundamental arena; the extra dimension would be, in a specific technical sense, emergent, and the ``bulk'' --- the three-dimensional interior we experience --- would be a kind of projection of the boundary description.

The most precise and mathematically developed realization of the holographic principle is the AdS/CFT correspondence, proposed by Juan Maldacena in 1997 within string theory.
In its best-studied form it relates a gravitational theory in a five-dimensional spacetime of a particular kind (anti-de~Sitter space, which has a negative cosmological constant) to a quantum field theory without gravity on that spacetime's four-dimensional boundary.
According to the correspondence, the two theories are completely equivalent: every fact about the five-dimensional bulk has an exact counterpart in the four-dimensional boundary theory.
The bulk is not an approximation to the boundary; the two are dual descriptions of the same physical reality.

\subsection*{Part C: Keeping the Two Ideas Apart}

It helps to sort what is established from what is not.
The Bekenstein--Hawking entropy is a theoretical result that nearly every specialist accepts.
It follows from well-tested physics applied in a regime where that physics should be reliable, and for certain special black holes in string theory it has been reproduced by directly counting microscopic states.
It has not been measured.
Hawking radiation from a black hole of stellar mass would be far colder than the cosmic microwave background, and detecting it is out of the question.
The holographic principle in its general form is a conjecture.
Raphael Bousso gave it a more careful formulation, adapted to curved and evolving spacetimes, at the end of the 1990s, and many physicists treat it as a guide to quantum gravity; but no one has derived it from a complete theory of quantum gravity, because no such theory is in hand.
AdS/CFT is also a conjecture rather than a theorem.
It has passed a very large number of nontrivial checks and has become one of the most productive tools in theoretical physics, yet it is formulated for spacetimes with a negative cosmological constant, and the universe we live in appears to have a positive one.
Whether anything like it holds for our cosmos is an open question.
The information problem itself has seen real progress in recent years, with calculations indicating that information does come out in the radiation, but the full mechanism is still debated, and none of this has been tested by experiment.

A second clarification concerns the kind of wholeness involved, and here I have to correct a loose way of speaking that is common in popular accounts.
The holographic principle does not say that every small patch of the boundary encodes the whole interior.
It says that the boundary as a whole does.
In AdS/CFT, a small region of the boundary determines only a correspondingly limited region of the bulk lying close to it; to reconstruct what happens deep in the interior you need a large portion of the boundary.
Work from the mid-2010s added a refinement: the way the bulk is written into the boundary has the structure of a quantum error-correcting code.
Information about a given point in the interior is stored redundantly and non-locally, so that it can be recovered from several different large boundary regions and cannot be found in any small one.
That is a genuine sense in which the information is spread out rather than located.
But it is not the property of the photographic plate, where any fragment yields the whole scene at lower resolution.
The holographic principle is, at bottom, a claim about dimensional reduction and about how much information a region can hold.
Its name comes from an analogy with optics, not from a shared mechanism.

Bohm's proposal sits closer to the optical case.
In the implicate order each region is supposed to enfold the whole, as each fragment of the plate does.
So the two physical ideas differ in status (a metaphysical reading of quantum mechanics against a conjecture with a precise mathematical realization), in domain (ordinary quantum phenomena against quantum gravity), and in structure (the whole enfolded in every region against a volume encoded on its boundary).
They share a word and a family resemblance, and I do not want to trade on the word.

What they genuinely have in common is weaker than identity and stronger than coincidence.
In both, the information that specifies the whole is not parceled out point by point, with each location holding only its own local fraction.
Both deny that the world's information is stored the way bricks are stored in a wall.
That shared denial, and not any claim that the two ideas are the same, is the thread I will follow through the rest of the chapter.

\section{The Process-Philosophical View: Universal Presence in Prehension}
\label{sec:ch11-process}

Whitehead's account of prehension contains, on close reading, a structural claim about holographic order that he reached through philosophical argument rather than physics.

Every actual occasion, in its concrescence, prehends the relevant portions of the objective past --- the settled, determinate reality of prior occasions that is available for prehension.
Whitehead insists that this prehension is, in principle, universal: every actual occasion has some relationship to every prior occasion in its light cone, however slight or filtered that relationship may be in practice~\citep{Whitehead1929}.

This universality is not a casual claim.
It is a structural consequence of how relational ontology works.
If an occasion is constituted by its prehensions, and its prehensions reach back through the chain of prior occasions to the most distant past, then in principle the occasion's relational heritage includes the entirety of prior actuality.
The causal past is not merely a set of nearby events that happened to influence this occasion.
It is the entire web of relational inheritance that reaches back to the beginning.

In practice, of course, near occasions dominate over distant ones; strong prehensions dominate over weak ones.
The causal past is not equally present in each new occasion.
But the structural claim is that the whole of what has been is, in some degree and some mode, present in each new occasion of becoming.
No occasion is purely local.
Each is a perspective on the whole.

Whitehead also supplies the counterpart of the hologram's loss of resolution.
A past occasion does not enter a new one with the whole of its inner complexity intact.
It is \emph{objectified}, in his term: felt under a perspective, with some of its features taken up and others dismissed from relevance~\citep{Whitehead1929}.
The new occasion has a definite relation to every item in its actual world, but most of those relations are vague and trivial, and only a few are sharp.
This is the process version of the distinction between presence and fidelity that I drew from the photographic plate.
Everything prior is present; what differs from occasion to occasion, and from one inherited item to another, is how much of it is taken up and how clearly.
A drop of rain and a human moment of reflection inherit the same universe.
They do not inherit it at the same resolution.

The analogy to holographic encoding is striking.
In the holographic principle, the boundary encodes the information of the bulk: each small area of the boundary encodes not just what is locally near but the entire interior volume.
In Whitehead's prehensive universality, each actual occasion encodes not just what is immediately adjacent but the entire causal past.
The occasion is a local condensation of a universal relational field.

Whitehead uses the phrase ``the universe as attained'' to describe the objective past available for prehension~\citep{Whitehead1929}.
The universe, as attained up to this moment, is present in each new occasion's prehensive inheritance.
The occasion is not a point in space that receives influences from its immediate neighbors.
It is a perspective on the whole of attained reality, taking in the universe from this particular standpoint.

This is close to what Leibniz meant by his claim that each monad ``mirrors'' the whole universe from its particular perspective.
Whitehead was in dialogue with Leibniz throughout his life, and the structural similarity is not accidental.
But where Leibniz's monads are windowless --- they contain the mirror image of the universe within themselves independently of actual causal connection --- Whitehead's occasions are constituted by prehensive relations.
The whole is present not because it is somehow built into the occasion in advance, but because the occasion is constituted by relational inheritance that reaches, through the chain of causes, across the whole of the past.

The analogy with the physics has a limit that I should name.
Whitehead's inheritance runs along time: an occasion takes in its past, not its contemporaries and not its future.
The holographic principle concerns a boundary and the region of spacetime it encloses, and Bohm's implicate order is meant to lie beneath the ordinary distinction between here and there altogether.
So the three are not describing the same relation.
What they share is the pattern of a whole that is available at a locus which is not itself the whole, a pattern each of them realizes along a different axis.

The process-philosophical version of holographic order is thus grounded in relational ontology: every actual occasion inherits from the entire objective past because every actual occasion is a node in a web of relational inheritance that has no local boundary.
The whole is present in each part because each part is constituted by relations to the whole.

\section{The Bahá'í View: The Essence of Light Within}
\label{sec:ch11-bahai}

The \bahai writings contain passages that make claims about the presence of the whole within human beings that are, at first encounter, easily read as simple spiritual metaphor.
When examined structurally, they turn out to be making a specific ontological claim.

The most striking single statement is from the Hidden Words of \Bahaullah:

\begin{quote}
Within thee have I placed the essence of My light.~\citep[Arabic no.~12]{HiddenWords}
\end{quote}

The word ``essence'' is doing significant work here.
In the \bahai metaphysical framework, the divine light is not a substance that can be divided up and parceled out.
It is a unity.
To say that the essence of the light has been placed within is not to say that a portion or a fragment or a subset of the light is within.
It is to say that the whole --- the undivided unity of the light --- is interior to the human soul.

This is the holographic structure.
The whole is not divided to produce the local instance.
The whole is present, in its wholeness, in each of its expressions.
The human soul does not contain a piece of the divine reality.
It is constituted as an expression of the whole of divine reality, in the particular mode and degree appropriate to its relational constitution.

\Bahaullah develops this further in the Seven Valleys and throughout the mystical writings.
The soul is described as a mirror: but not as a mirror that reflects a part of the sun.
A mirror reflects the whole sun.
The image of the sun in the mirror is not a fragment.
It is the complete sun, in the form available to this particular mirror, with its particular size, quality, and orientation.
The difference between a small tarnished mirror and a large polished mirror is not that one reflects part of the sun and the other reflects all of it.
Both reflect the whole sun.
The difference is in the clarity and completeness of the reflection --- in the degree to which the mirror's own limitations distort or diminish the image.

The structural point is that the sun itself is not divided.
What varies is the instrument.
The reality is one; its local expressions vary by the nature of the instrument that expresses it.

\AbdulBaha uses the same image in a talk given in Philadelphia in June 1912, and he adds a qualification that is essential for reading it correctly.
The Sun of Reality, he says, is one and shines upon the whole phenomenal world, while each kingdom receives its light according to its capacity; the perfect human soul is like a mirror in which that Sun is reflected~\citep[Talk~62]{PUP}.
He then rules out the reading that would turn the image into a doctrine of divine indwelling.
The reflection does not mean that the Sun has left its place in the heavens or that its essential being has entered the mirror, ``for there is neither entrance nor exit for the reality of Divinity''~\citep[Talk~62]{PUP}.
The whole is present in the mirror as an image, fully and at the mirror's resolution, while the sun remains where it is.
This is the relation of the photographic plate to the scene it records: the plate carries the whole scene without the objects being inside it, and nothing was taken from the objects to put the image there.

The \bahai writings also develop this theme through the concept of the names and attributes of God, and here the texts are more differentiated than a simple holographic reading would suggest.
The divine attributes --- life, knowledge, power, will, love, and so on --- are not discrete fragments of a divine substance.
They are aspects of a unity.
\Bahaullah teaches that every created thing, each according to its capacity, is a sign of the revelation of God, so that nothing in the universe fails to reflect something of that splendour~\citep{GleaningsBahaullah}.
% TODO: add Gleanings section numbers (likely XXVII for the quotation; XC/XCIII for the paraphrase). Wording verified in sources/bahai-sacred-writings.pdf.
But in a passage on the purpose of creation he draws a distinction.
Upon the inmost reality of each created thing, he writes, God ``hath shed the light of one of His names''; upon the reality of man, however, God ``hath focused the radiance of all of His names and attributes, and made it a mirror of His own Self''~\citep{GleaningsBahaullah}.

This matters for the convergence, and I want to state it plainly rather than smooth it over.
The writings do not say that a stone reflects the whole in the way a human soul does.
They describe a gradient.
Every created thing reflects the one light, and the human reality is the place where the full range of attributes is brought to a focus.
In the terms I took from the photographic plate, presence is universal and fidelity is graded.
The strongest form of the holographic claim, the whole in its full range available within the part, is made of the human soul in particular.

``Spirit, mind, soul, and the powers of sight and hearing are but one single reality which hath manifold expressions owing to the diversity of its instruments''~\citep[para.~35]{SummonsLordHosts}.
The single reality is one.
Its expressions are many.
But each expression is an expression of the whole reality, not of a part of it.
What varies is the instrument --- the mode of expression --- not the reality expressed.

This is why the \bahai concept of human potential is not, at bottom, a quantitative concept.
It is not that some people have more of the divine attributes and some have less, as if the attributes were a resource to be apportioned.
It is that the whole of reality --- the complete set of divine attributes in their indivisible unity --- is available, in principle, to every human soul.
What varies is the degree to which the soul has been polished, clarified, made transparent to the light that shines within it.

\AbdulBaha develops this in \emph{Some Answered Questions} when discussing the human spirit~\citep[Part 4]{SAQ}.
The human spirit is not a portion of the divine spirit.
It is a reflection of it --- complete, in its mode.
The soul as a complete mirror of a complete sun, not as a fragment of either.

The \bahai writings also make a claim that connects directly to the question of the whole being present in the part at the universal scale, not just at the scale of individual souls.
\AbdulBaha's statement that ``all created things are connected one to another by a linkage complete and perfect''~\citep[\S 21]{SelectionsAbdul} is sometimes read as a statement about nonlocal correlations, but the letter says nothing about distance (Chapter~\ref{ch:nonlocal-interconnection}).
I read it instead as a claim about the holographic structure of creation: that no created thing is purely local.
Each thing is connected to every other, not as an element in a causal chain that happens to extend far, but as an expression of a relational unity that is complete in each of its expressions.

This is the holographic insight in ontological language: the universe is not a collection of separable parts that happen to be related.
It is a unity that expresses itself completely in each of its parts, each part being a perspective on the whole from a particular relational location.

\section{The Structural Correspondence (With Appropriate Caution)}
\label{sec:ch11-convergence}

I have already flagged the appropriate caution: this is the most analogical of the six convergences.
Part C of the physics section set out what that means.
The holographic principle is a conjecture about quantum gravity with one precise realization, AdS/CFT, in spacetimes unlike our own, and it concerns a boundary encoding a volume rather than every fragment encoding the whole.
Bohm's implicate order is a metaphysical reading of quantum mechanics with no predictive content of its own.
Extending either to organisms, minds, or souls involves a move that the physics does not itself sanction.
I am not claiming that the \bahai writings about the essence of light within, or the process-philosophical claim of universal prehension, are described by the holographic principle in the technical sense.
That claim would overreach.

What I am claiming is a structural correspondence.
The same abstract relationship --- between a whole that is present in each of its parts, not through division but through holographic encoding --- appears independently in all three traditions.
The quantum side instantiates this in one way (information encoding on boundaries, enfolding in implicate order).
The process side instantiates it in another (universal prehensive inheritance of the whole objective past).
The \bahai side instantiates it in a third (the undivided divine reality present in each soul, each created thing connected to every other by a complete linkage).

The three are not identical.
But they are structurally homologous: the same abstract pattern of whole-in-every-part appears in all three, implemented through different mechanisms and at different levels of description.

This is worth noting even with the caveats, because the common structure is unusual.
The default picture of the relationship between wholes and parts in both ordinary experience and classical physics is additive: the whole is the sum of the parts; the parts contain fractions of the whole; to get more, you add more.
All three traditions are denying this picture.
They are all saying that the relationship between the whole and its local expressions is non-additive in a specific way: the whole is available in each expression, not as a fraction, but as a complete presence at a particular resolution or depth.

The \bahai mirror analogy captures this precisely.
The mirror does not contain a fraction of the sun.
It shows the whole sun, at the resolution its surface can sustain.
A larger mirror gathers more of the sun's light, but not a different sun.
The sun is whole in each mirror.
What varies is the mirror's capacity.

\subsection*{The Strongest Objection}

The strongest objection runs as follows.
The phrase ``the whole in every part'' has here been stretched over at least four different relations: a fragment of a plate that reconstructs a scene, a boundary that encodes a volume, a present occasion that inherits its past, and a mirror that shows a sun.
Loosen a phrase enough and it will fit anything.
The same pattern turns up in many places this book does not discuss, from Leibniz's mirroring monads to the jewelled net of Huayan Buddhism, which suggests that it may record a habit of the human imagination rather than a discovery about the world.
A convergence this easy to find, the objector concludes, is evidence of nothing.

I think the objection is partly right, and the fair response is to concede what it gets right and then narrow the claim until it can bear weight.
Of the physical ideas, the holographic principle is the weakest leg here, for the reason given in Part C: it concerns dimensional reduction, not fragments that carry the whole.
It belongs in this chapter as a second instance of non-local encoding, not as evidence about the constitution of the soul.
And the recurrence of the pattern in human thought cuts both ways; from the armchair I cannot tell whether it reflects a feature of the world or a predilection of minds like ours.

The narrower claim has three parts.
The whole is present in the part without being divided; the fidelity of that presence varies with the capacity of the part; and the whole is neither located in nor exhausted by any part.
That is more specific than saying everything is connected, and ordinary part-whole relations fail it: a slice of cake satisfies none of the three conditions.
The optical hologram meets all three.
Bohm's implicate order meets the first and, through its degrees of unfolding, arguably the others.
Whitehead meets all three through objectification under a perspective.
The \bahai writings state all three explicitly, the third in \AbdulBaha's insistence that the sun does not enter the mirror.
What converges is a definite shape, and the weight this chapter carries in the book's argument is correspondingly modest: it adds a fifth instance to four stronger ones rather than standing on its own.

It is worth saying directly what the convergence does not claim.
It does not claim that the universe is literally a hologram in Bohm's sense or in Susskind's, or that the soul is the boundary surface of anything.
It does not claim that the physics of black holes bears on consciousness, or that any experiment tests the \bahai teaching.
It does not claim that God is contained in the soul; the texts themselves exclude that.
Nor does it claim that every soul reflects the whole equally.

I leave open whether Bohm's implicate order and the holographic principle describe the same physical structure, related structures, or merely homologous ones, and the same question applies to their relation to universal prehension and to the \bahai claims.
What I do not leave open is the narrower point: in all three traditions, the whole is present in each part in a mode that is not fractional.

\begin{convergencemoment}{Holographic Order: The Whole in Every Part}
David Bohm's implicate order~\citep{Bohm1980} proposes that the explicate world of separate, locally interacting things is the unfolding of a deeper implicate structure in which the whole universe is enfolded in every local region.
The hologram is the analogy: every fragment of a holographic film contains the whole image.
The local is not a fragment of the global; it is a specific unfolding of the whole.

The holographic principle~\citep{tHooft1993,Susskind1995}, motivated by the physics of black holes and quantum gravity, proposes that the information content of any spatial volume is encoded on its boundary surface.
The interior is, in a precise technical sense, a holographic projection of the boundary.
The information is written on the surface as a whole rather than stored point by point in the volume.

In process philosophy~\citep{Whitehead1929}, every actual occasion prehends the whole of the objective past, in some degree and in some mode.
Each occasion is a perspective on the whole --- a local condensation of a universal relational field.
No occasion is purely local; the entire causal past is present in its relational constitution.

In the \bahai writings~\citep[Arabic no.~12]{HiddenWords}, ``Within thee have I placed the essence of My light.''
The divine reality is not divided to produce the local expression.
The whole is present in each soul, as the whole sun is present in each mirror.
What varies is the instrument; the reality expressed is undivided.
``Spirit, mind, soul, and the powers of sight and hearing are but one single reality which hath manifold expressions owing to the diversity of its instruments''~\citep[para.~35]{SummonsLordHosts}.

One structure, with appropriate acknowledgment of where the analogy thins: the whole is locally available in every part.
The relationship between the whole and its expressions is not fractional but holographic.
The part does not contain a fraction of the whole.
The part is a particular expression of the whole --- complete, at the resolution the part can sustain.
This non-additive relationship between whole and part appears independently, in structurally homologous form, across all three traditions.
